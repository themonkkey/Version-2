1. Why this matters in Andhra Pradesh

Cotton stalk is not just waste — it is a district-level feedstock for energy, soil improvement and incomes.

13.0 lakh bales
AP cotton pressing estimate for 2023–24 in CAI table
5 old districts

Residue → value

stalks can become pellets, briquettes, biochar and carbon credits

The usual end-of-season problem: stalks occupy fields, removal is laborious, burning causes local air pollution, and farmers receive no value.

The opportunity: organize post-harvest collection through FPOs/SHGs, chip within villages, and link to nearby pellet/briquette units or industrial users.

District lens

Cotton clusters have concentrated, seasonal feedstock.

Ginning/power/brick/thermal users create local demand.

District administration can reduce open burning while adding farm income.

4. Logistics model: the missing link solved

CIRCOT identifies logistics—not pellet technology alone—as the key barrier to cotton stalk utilization.

CIRCOT model

Uproot stalks from field with tractor-operated V-type uprooter.

Sun dry the uprooted stalks near the field.

Shred/chip with tractor-operated shredder.

Transport chipped stalks to pellet/briquette plant within a 50 km radius.

Why it fits AP: existing tractors, custom-hiring centres, FPO networks and ginning clusters can be used to organize the seasonal collection window.

8. District implementation model

Create a reliable biomass supply chain before scaling pellet or biochar capacity.

FPO/SHG

Plant/Buyer

End users

District administration role

Notify pilot mandals and create residue aggregation calendar.

Converge Agriculture, DRDA/SHG, Pollution Control, Industries and Energy departments.

Facilitate buyer MoUs and common weighing/quality protocol.

Operating safeguards

Storage yards away from houses and power lines.

Moisture specification to prevent fungal/fire risk.

Transparent grade deductions and payment timelines.

No diversion of essential fodder where relevant.

Use one nodal officer and one district dashboard. Without tonnage, payment and buyer data, scaling will fail.

Source basis: ICAR-CIRCOT logistics model and URJA community/FPO model.

10. 90-day pilot plan for one district

Aim: prove tonnage, payments, quality and buyer acceptance in one collection season.

Days 0–15

Preparation

Select mandals, map cotton acreage, identify FPO/SHG aggregators and buyer/off-taker.

Days 16–30

Readiness

Finalize prices, service charges, storage yards, machines and safety protocol.

Days 31–60

Field operations

Uproot, dry, chip and transport stalks; digital weighment and farmer payment.

Days 61–75

Processing

Pellet/briquette trial production; test moisture, bulk density and combustion use.

Days 76–90

Scale decision

Publish pilot report; approve next-season scale-up and financing needs.

Pilot target suggestion: 2–3 mandal clusters, 1 anchor buyer, 1 machine-service group per cluster and daily tonnage/payment tracking.

Source basis: implementation plan adapted from CIRCOT logistics steps and URJA social-enterprise model.

11. Ask from district officials

A small, well-governed pilot can unlock a statewide cotton-stalk bioenergy program.

Approve district pilot

Identify 2–3 mandal clusters and one nodal officer for the 90-day pilot.

Convene partners

Bring FPOs, SHGs, CHCs, pellet units, ginners, industrial users and KVK/technical experts to one table.

Enable market signal

Issue transparent price, quality, storage and payment protocol; sign buyer MoUs before collection starts.

Next step

Prepare a one-page district cotton-stalk resource map.

Select pilot villages and machine owners before harvest end.

Collect data from day one: tonnes, moisture, payments, costs and buyers.

Use the pilot report to propose district/state budget, CSR and carbon finance support.
